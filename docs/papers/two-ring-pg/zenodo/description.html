Reading the Curve, Not Counting the Points: A Consistent, Stable Petrov–Galerkin Curvature Force for Discrete Yang–Mills Dynamics on the Poincaré Homology Sphere
Bee Rosa Davis, Davis Geometric. November 2026.

The Davis–Wilson programme asks whether lattice gauge dynamics computed from coarse edge data reproduce the continuum flow they discretize. Its first requirement is instantaneous: the discrete force, evaluated on the exact edge integrals of a smooth connection and tested against every discrete variation, must converge to the continuum force. On shape-regular meshes of the Poincaré homology sphere S3/2I, the lowest-order Whitney force fails this: its error stalls at 26% under refinement. This paper shows that the floor comes from how curvature is represented, not from the data, and removes it.

What this version adds

The first version established the two-ring recovery for an Abelian control and carried the recovery and the instantaneous force to SU(2). This version takes the SU(2) force further, tests it in time, and tightens the presentation.

  • Numerical range of the SU(2) force. Eigenvalues alone do not rule out transient growth in a non-normal operator. Whitened by the native mass, the two-ring force's numerical range has its left edge at −4.66 at n = 2, against −4.68 for the native force and −19,993 for the one-ring force. Its only growth is the field's own motion; the operator's strong non-normality lives in fast, strongly damped modes.
  • The SU(2) force in time. With the Berger metric (3, 1, 1) following its Ricci flow over t ∈ [0, 0.02], the links are integrated from their exact initial values and compared with the exact links of the flowed metric. The two-ring force ends 13.8%, 1.76% and 0.87% off the exact trajectory (error over distance travelled) at n = 1, 2, 3, against 7.01%, 2.21% and 1.07% for the native force: native leads at n = 1, two rings lead from n = 2 on, in raw and gauge-invariant measures alike. Halving the time step or changing the quadrature moves the error by well under a tenth. Freezing the metric at its initial value leaves an error of about 8% that does not shrink with the mesh, so the lattice demonstrably follows the moving background. The one-ring force diverges at the two-ring step, as its spectrum predicts.
  • Why the native force does not stall in time. Although its instantaneous error stays near 25% at every level, the native trajectory error keeps falling. A first-order linear filter model predicts every measured trajectory error, for both forces, to within 6%, and explains it: at n = 2 only 0.85% of the native force's error lies in slow directions (7.6% for two rings). The 25% is real but stiff, and the dynamics damps it within the window. The paper records this as a failed prediction and its measured explanation.
  • A Cayley (Crank–Nicolson) time step. The finest level is reached with the Lie implicit midpoint rule built on the Cayley map, which keeps every update exactly on SU(2). It was checked before use: its gap from the exponential map falls as |v|3/12, its observed time order is 1.99, and with eight steps it reproduces the fourth-order Runge–Kutta–Munthe-Kaas errors at n = 1, 2 to within 1.5% for a fifth to a third of the force evaluations. The step comes from the companion paper Three Ways Across a Turn (doi:10.5281/zenodo.24375338), where the Crank–Nicolson step is the tangent reading of a turn.
  • An exact defect derivative. Converging the implicit step required differentiating each face defect in closed form; a finite-difference derivative had left the force noisy at about 10−10. With the exact derivative the research force matches the native Rust engine's gradient to 8 × 10−15.
  • The third level. At n = 3 (11,358 unknowns) every step converged and the flow gates hold: two-ring error 0.87%, observed order 1.74 from n = 2, step halving 0.75%; the frozen-metric gap, measured exactly from saved states, is ten times the moving run's error. One forecast, made without a gate, is reported as missed: the two-ring error did not fall faster than the native one between n = 2 and n = 3 (orders 1.74 and 1.80).
  • Presentation. A new subtitle (the paper now covers SU(2), not only an Abelian control), a shorter abstract, the SU(2) material organised as recovery, force and flow in time, and corrections to statements that later results had overtaken.

Main results

Abelian control. A closed linear curvature recovered on each cell's face-star and tested against the original Whitney variations gives a consistent Petrov–Galerkin force. In its minimal one-ring form the recovery is exactly determined: it amplifies rough data by a median 125–252, and its operator has 489 of 961 physical modes with negative real part at n = 2. Recovering on a two-ring neighbourhood by equality-constrained least squares keeps the fit exact on closed linear curvature, cuts the amplification to a mesh-uniform median of 30.5–35.2, and puts every decomposed physical eigenvalue (n ≤ 3) in the right half-plane, with the smallest at the control's true value of 4; at n = 1, 2 the symmetric part is positive definite, so the semi-discrete flow is dissipative. The force error falls to 6.22%, 3.83%, 2.79% and 1.45% at n = 1, 2, 3, 5 (observed orders 0.73, 0.80, 1.29) and passes the unchanged BW05 gate. Nothing is fitted and no data are added: only the size of the neighbourhood the reading consults changes. Two to four well-chosen second-ring cells per cell already suffice; the same number chosen at random is unstable. The force is not the gradient of an energy, and the paper shows quantitatively why every symmetrized variant fails.

SU(2). With neighbouring holonomies turned into register by link transport, the covariant recovery converges at second order on the Levi-Civita fields of the round and a Berger metric. On the Berger field the native SU(2) force stays 25.5% wrong, while the two-ring force falls to 2.51% at n = 5 and passes BW05, with no rough modes in its linearised spectrum (the one-ring force has 207, then 1,451), a numerical range matching the native one, and the time behaviour summarised above.

What is not claimed

  • No Ricci-flow claim. The targets are the instantaneous Yang–Mills force and, for SU(2), one short window of the flow on one metric. The results are a prerequisite for a lattice Davis–Wilson correspondence, not an instance of one; the programme's release gate stays open.
  • The SU(2) recovery, force and flow are research scripts, not engine verbs. The flow is a research replica of the programme's flow gate at n ≤ 3; stability and the numerical range are measured at n ≤ 2.
  • The force is not reciprocal (not the gradient of an energy). An accurate, stable and reciprocal force is left open.

Reproducibility

Every computation runs in GIGI, an open-source geometric engine (PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0): github.com/nurdymuny/gigi. Native Rust transport and mass assembly are checked against the full 120-fold cover; the two-ring recovery is a native query verb, RECOVER_CURVATURE, that matches the research code cell by cell on every mesh. The manuscript source, figure and table scripts are in docs/papers/two-ring-pg/, and every number traces to a hash-pinned receipt under research/, with the specification for each experiment written before its measurement. This version corresponds to commit 1252532 on branch feat/reading-the-curve.

Companion paper

Three Ways Across a Turn: Chord, Arc and Tangent (B. R. Davis, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24375338) reads one turn three ways: the chord, which lattice gauge theory has used since Wilson; the arc, which the continuum uses; and the outside path along the tangents, whose leg is the Cayley parameter. It explains why the chord–arc gap is negligible on these meshes and supplies the Cayley time step used here.
