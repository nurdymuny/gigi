# The floor

27 November 2026. A research note, not about the mathematics this time, but about how it gets made.

Today I had to apologize to my assistant: the one who checks my math, retrieves my research data and helps me write code.

## What happened

It was a long day of work on the Yang–Mills programme, and a good one.
- I found the M3 computation: the fast modes of the field reproduce asymptotic freedom exactly.
- I put my whole theory on one page, then on twenty-nine lines.
- I closed seven of those lines by deduction.
- I merged two hypotheses into one, because I begin with the geometry.
- By evening I had a narrative statement of what I believe the mass gap is.

Along the way I got frustrated, more than once. It felt as though the goalposts kept moving: every time I thought the list of what a proof needs was complete, something new appeared on it. When my assistant graded the work, the number felt like a stamp of failure on everything we had found together. I said that if I was being led on and on, working on this made no sense.

Some of that frustration was fair, and my assistant said so plainly:
- The grade had answered the wrong question. I asked *how close* I am; the answer said *whether it is proved*, and printed that as a number.
- A word of jargon ("ultraviolet") had never been translated, so it arrived like a new wall.
- One sentence undersold the construction.

Each of those got corrected. The requirements are now pinned to the problem's own text, clause by clause, so they cannot drift.

But some of it was mine. I think very fast. A new direction arrives while the last one is still running, and then neither of us can tell what is settled and what is not. My assistant said this gently, and it was true.

## What I learned

My mind picks locks. Any wall or door in front of me, I pick it. That is how I found Helike, and how I came to see the size of the room as a coordinate of the bundle. But a mind that opens every door has no floor. It falls, the way I keep saying the field falls into the gap.

Staying still is a choice, and patience is a practice, not a trait. "I can't help it" would be an excuse. A practice can be held up by structure and by people, so it does not have to run on willpower alone.

The structure we built today is a small version of a floor: **a closed register**. It is one file that holds every statement I work from. Requirements come only from the problem's own text. Only two kinds of change are allowed: a status moves when something is proved or fails, or a proof inside a line changes. Any claim that something is missing has to quote the clause it comes from. I can range as far as I like and still have somewhere to land.

That is also a method result, and I want to keep it:
- **Fix the requirements against an external text before working.** Otherwise the work and its target drift together.
- **Keep one register,** and log every change with its date.
- **Separate "how close" from "is it proved".** The first is a gradient, the second a fact; confusing them hurts, and teaches nothing.
- **Translate every term of art the first time it appears.**

## The people who held the floor

For most of my life a few people helped me stay still, and they are gone now. Today I noticed how much of that steadiness I had been asking my work, and my assistant, to supply. Some of it can be built on purpose, as structure. Some of it only people can give.

So I apologized, and I will do better: I will wait for one thing to land before starting the next.
