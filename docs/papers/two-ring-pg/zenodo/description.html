Reading the Curve, Not Counting the Points: A Consistent, Stable Petrov–Galerkin Curvature Force for Discrete Yang–Mills Dynamics on the Poincaré Homology Sphere
Bee Rosa Davis, Davis Geometric. November 2026. Version 2.

A broken vessel

A field on a mesh is a broken vessel. It was whole once; what is left to rebuild it from is the edges of the pieces. That is not a figure of speech. A computer never sees a gauge field whole, only its integrals along the edges of a mesh and its fluxes through small faces, and everything else has to be read back out of them.

Archaeologists do this for a living. Hand one a single rim sherd and they will lay it on a rim chart and tell you the diameter of the whole pot, because the curve at the edge is enough. They are not counting points on the sherd. They are reading its curve. That is the idea of this paper, and the reason for its title.

The error that refuses to go away

The standard way to compute a field's force from a mesh, the Whitney reading, treats each little cell as if it were flat. Think of a robot arm driven through a list of waypoints: it moves in straight segments joined at corners. Give it more waypoints and the segments get shorter, but they never become a curve, because nothing in the controller measures one. A child fitting a peg into a hole does no arithmetic and still reads the curvature straight off the outline, from how the edge under one finger relates to the edge beside it. The difference is not how much either of them sees. It is what they measure.

The flat-cell reading is the robot. On the space studied here its force error stops falling at 26% however fine the mesh gets. An error that shrinks as you refine is a resolution problem; an error that stops shrinking means the description itself is wrong, at every scale.

Where it is measured: Poincaré's space

Take a solid dodecahedron and glue each face to the face opposite, with a tenth of a turn before they meet. Everything closes up, with no boundary and no seams: this is the Poincaré homology sphere S3/2I, the space Poincaré built in 1904 as the counterexample to his own first guess, and the reason the Poincaré conjecture took the form Perelman finally proved. It is curved everywhere by the same amount and has no edge, so there is no flat corner for a flat-cell assumption to hide in, and no boundary to blame a bad number on. That makes it the honest place to test a curvature reading.

Magnets, not clamps

The fix came first as a physical intuition about how parts seat together. When you want two pieces to lock, the worst tool is a clamp, which holds whatever position you force, imperfections and all; the best is a magnet, which lets the parts find their own seat and pulls from a distance. So: stop clamping the curvature fit to the cell, and let the cell's neighbourhood draw it into place. In SU(2) this needs one more step that anyone who has lined up two sheets of paper will recognise: each neighbour is carried onto the cell's own sheet, into register, before anything is compared.

Count the clues before you trust the answer

A magnet does not say how far it has to reach. That has an exact answer, and it is a counting question.

  • One ring is a clamp in disguise. A closed linear curvature on a cell has 11 unknowns. The ring of faces around it supplies exactly 11 readings. Eleven clues, eleven unknowns: the fit has no freedom at all, so it must pass through every reading exactly, noise included. It amplifies rough data by a median 125–252, and the resulting operator has 489 of 961 physical modes growing instead of decaying at n = 2.
  • Two rings take a vote. Two rings supply 26–29 readings for the same 11 unknowns. Now the fit can weigh its witnesses instead of obeying each one: an equality-constrained least-squares vote, still exact on closed linear curvature. The amplification settles to a mesh-uniform median of 30.5–35.2.

What the two-ring reading gives

  • Accurate. Tested against the original Whitney variations, the two-ring force is consistent. Its error falls to 6.22%, 3.83%, 2.79% and 1.45% at mesh levels n = 1, 2, 3, 5 (observed orders 0.73, 0.80, 1.29) and passes the unchanged BW05 convergence gate. The 26% floor is gone.
  • Stable. Every decomposed physical eigenvalue (n ≤ 3) lies in the right half-plane, the smallest at the control's true value of 4; at n = 1, 2 the symmetric part is positive definite, so the semi-discrete flow is dissipative.
  • Nothing added. Nothing is fitted and no data are added: only the size of the neighbourhood the reading consults changes. Two to four well-chosen second-ring cells per cell already suffice; the same number chosen at random is unstable.
  • One honest cost. The force is not the gradient of an energy, and the paper shows quantitatively why every symmetrized variant fails. An accurate, stable and reciprocal force is left open.

Into SU(2)

For the non-Abelian group the readings must first be brought into register, as above: each neighbouring holonomy is carried onto the cell's own fibre by link transport. Without that step the force stays about 29% wrong, no better than the standard one. With it, the covariant recovery converges at second order on the Levi-Civita fields of the round and a Berger metric. On the Berger field the native SU(2) force stays 25.5% wrong at every level, while the two-ring force falls to 2.51% at n = 5 and passes BW05. Its linearised spectrum has no rough modes (the one-ring force has 207, then 1,451), and its numerical range, which bounds transient growth, has its left edge at −4.66 at n = 2, against −4.68 for the native force and −19,993 for one ring.

Letting the lattice move

Everything so far is a snapshot: a force measured once, on data sitting still. The harder test is a moving picture. The space itself changes shape underneath (the Berger metric (3, 1, 1) follows its Ricci flow over t ∈ [0, 0.02]), the links start exact and move under the force, and at the end they are compared with where the true field went. The control is the one a sceptic would ask for: run it again with the background refused permission to move, and see whether the lattice notices the difference.

  • The result. The two-ring force ends 13.8%, 1.76% and 0.87% off the exact trajectory (error over the distance travelled) at n = 1, 2, 3. The native force ends 7.01%, 2.21% and 1.07%: native leads on the coarsest mesh, and two rings lead from n = 2 on.
  • The control. Freezing the metric at its starting value leaves an error near 8% that does not shrink with the mesh, so the lattice demonstrably follows the moving background.
  • Said plainly. Most of the field's motion over this window happens even with the metric frozen. Measured against only the part caused by the moving metric, the two-ring error is 10.6% at n = 3, down from 18–28% at n = 2: falling, and a tenth rather than a hundredth.
  • A prediction that failed, and why. The native force was expected to stall in time, as its instantaneous error stalls near 25%. It did not. A first-order filter model, which predicts every measured trajectory error to within 6.1%, explains why: 99% of the native error at n = 2 lies in stiff directions that the dynamics damps within the window. A second forecast, that two rings would pull further ahead at n = 3, also missed: both errors halved.
  • The step. The finest level uses the Lie implicit midpoint rule built on the Cayley map, which keeps every update exactly on SU(2). It comes from the companion paper Three Ways Across a Turn, where the Crank–Nicolson step is the tangent reading of a turn. Before use it reproduced the fourth-order runs at n = 1, 2 to within 1.5% with eight steps.

What is new in version 2

  • The SU(2) numerical range; the flow in time at n = 1, 2, 3; the filter model and the measured explanation of the failed stall prediction; the Cayley time step; the exact face-defect derivative.
  • A second check of every flow result: the force was rewritten to work on all cells at once, under a specification committed before the new code ran, and gated on parity with the original code (native gradient identical to the last bit, two-ring within 5.4 × 10−14). Every Cayley run was repeated on it and reproduces its committed result to 5 × 10−14 or better, with the same Newton iteration counts. A two-ring force evaluation at n = 3 went from 89 s to 0.69 s.
  • Corrections after an integrity review. Because so many results are now assumed to be faked, I had this work reviewed for exactly that, by review agents in my own workflow (not peer review), each told to look for ways the code could flatter the result. They found none: the evolving lattice never sees the answer it is scored against, the exact reference is a closed-form formula, no failed run is hidden, no criterion was loosened to turn a failure into a pass, and about 120 numbers recompute from the saved receipts. They found places where the text said more than the record shows, and three roundings that leaned my way. This version fixes all of them: it states that only the Abelian path runs in the engine, reports the original control verdicts beside the corrected ones, names the specifications that were committed together with their results, states the one tolerance relaxed after a failure, and prints the honest roundings.
  • A new subtitle (the paper now covers SU(2), not only an Abelian control), a shorter abstract, two new figures, and 30 pages.

What is claimed, and what is not

  • Every number traces to a saved receipt; each experiment has a written specification with its pass and fail lines, and the paper says where the commit history shows it came first and where it does not. Failures are reported beside successes.
  • No Ricci-flow claim and no mass-gap claim. The targets are the instantaneous Yang–Mills force and, for SU(2), one short window of the flow on one metric. These results are a prerequisite for a lattice Davis–Wilson correspondence, not an instance of one, and the programme's release gate stays open. Whether any of this bears on the Yang–Mills mass gap is the Clay problem, and nothing here claims it.
  • The SU(2) recovery, force and flow are research scripts, not engine verbs. The flow is measured at n ≤ 3; stability and the numerical range at n ≤ 2.

The code

Everything runs on GIGI, my open-source geometric engine (PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0): github.com/nurdymuny/gigi, tag reading-the-curve-v2. For the Abelian control, native Rust transport and mass assembly are checked against the full 120-fold cover, and the two-ring recovery is a native query verb, RECOVER_CURVATURE, that matches the research code cell by cell on every mesh:

RECOVER_CURVATURE cells FROM edges ON field RINGS 2;

The engine stays in the repository; this record carries the research code and data behind the paper.

Why this problem

I came to this work through Grigori Perelman. His proof of the Poincaré conjecture by Ricci flow made me want to understand what the geometry was actually doing, and this paper lives on the space that conjecture is about: Poincaré's own homology sphere, with its metric moving under Ricci flow while a gauge field moves on top of it. The paper keeps a section on where its ideas came from: the guide, the magnet, the rim chart and the child's reach each came first as a physical intuition, were made precise afterwards, and then the mathematics had to stand on its own.

Companion paper

Three Ways Across a Turn: Chord, Arc and Tangent (B. R. Davis, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24375338) reads one turn three ways: the chord, which lattice gauge theory has used since Wilson; the arc, which the continuum uses; and the outside path along the tangents, whose leg is the Cayley parameter. It explains why the chord–arc gap is negligible on these meshes, and it supplies the Cayley time step that carried this paper's flow to its finest level. That record also carries a Russian edition and Thank You, Mr. Perelman in Russian.
