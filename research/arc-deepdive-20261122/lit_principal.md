## 1. Kostant 1959: the citation itself

- **QUOTED (bibliographic).** B. Kostant, "The Principal Three-Dimensional Subgroup and the Betti Numbers of a Complex Simple Lie Group", Amer. J. Math. 81 (1959) 973–1032. It is reprinted in Kostant's *Collected Papers* (Springer 2009), pp. 130–189, DOI 10.1007/b94535_11. The reprint's abstract opens: "Let g be a complex simple Lie algebra and let G be the adjoint group of g." and "It is by now classical that the Poincaré polynomial p G(t) of G factors into the form". URL: https://econpapers.repec.org/bookchap/sprsprchp/978-0-387-09583-7_5f11.htm
- **UNVERIFIED.** I did not read the 1959 paper. JSTOR is paywalled and the abstract page cuts off mid-sentence. Everything below about Kostant's results comes from expositions that cite him, quoted verbatim. I do not quote Kostant himself.
- **UNVERIFIED.** The Betti-number theorem itself, P_G(t) = ∏(1 + t^{2m_i+1}), was not stated verbatim in any source I fetched. It is the paper's title result and I have no doubt it is the standard statement, but no fetched source says it word for word, so I do not state it as quoted fact.

## 2. Definition and construction (the grading element is ρ∨)

- **QUOTED (definition).** Hitchin (1992), §4: "A nilpotent element e ∈ g^c is called regular (or principal) if its centralizer is l-dimensional. … By the Jacobson-Morozov lemma, any nilpotent element can be embedded in a 3-dimensional simple subalgebra … generated by a semi-simple element x and nilpotent elements e and ẽ satisfying the relations: [x, e] = e; [x, ẽ] = −ẽ; [e, ẽ] = x. For a regular nilpotent, this is called a principal 3-dimensional subalgebra." N. J. Hitchin, "Lie groups and Teichmüller space", Topology 31 (1992) 449–473. URL: https://pagine.dm.unipi.it/~a019210/Hitchin_Lie%20group%20and%20Teichmuller%20theory.pdf (my notation is ẽ; the OCR'd text writes it as "Z"/"P").
- **QUOTED (equivalent definition).** Dalakov, §6.2: "The subalgebra a is called principal if a\{0} ⊂ g_reg." P. Dalakov, *Lectures on Higgs moduli and abelianisation*, arXiv:1609.00646. URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.00646
- **QUOTED (the grading element).** Baraglia, §2.1, constructs it explicitly: "Kostant [10] defines a subalgebra (unique up to conjugacy) called the principal 3-dimensional subalgebra of g. We construct the principal 3-dimensional subalgebra as follows. Let x = ½ Σ_{α∈Δ+} h_α". Here h_α is the coroot, so x is the half-sum of positive coroots, **ρ∨**. Then: "Lemma 2.1. For any β ∈ Π we have β(x) = 1." D. Baraglia, "Cyclic Higgs bundles and the affine Toda equations", arXiv:1011.6421. URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6421
- **QUOTED (normalization warning).** The MIT OCW 18.757 (Fall 2023) Lecture 17 notes use the doubled normalization, with neutral element "h = Σ c_i h_i = 2ρ∨". URL: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-757-representations-of-lie-groups-fall-2023/mit18_757_f23_lec17.pdf. So the "charge" of a root vector is its height in the x = ρ∨ convention (Hitchin, Baraglia) and twice its height in the h = 2ρ∨ convention (MIT, Dalakov). **Any statement that "her charge is 1" has to name its convention.**
- **PROVED (SU(3) check).** In sl(3), the positive coroots are diag(1,−1,0), diag(0,1,−1) and diag(1,0,−1). Half their sum is diag(1,0,−1). So **H = diag(1,0,−1) is exactly ρ∨ for sl(3)**, in Baraglia/Hitchin normalization. That confirms the STRUCTURAL tag in the arc note.

## 3. The principal grading counts roots by height

- **QUOTED.** Baraglia, §2.1: "Given a root λ ∈ Δ, let λ = Σ_{α∈Π} n_α α. Then we define the Π-height of λ to be the integer height(λ) = Σ_{α∈Π} n_α. The above lemma shows that for y ∈ g_α, we have [x, y] = height(α)y, that is x is the grading element corresponding to the gradation of g by height." URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6421
- **QUOTED.** Hitchin (1992), §4: "the eigenvalues of ad x, the semisimple element of sl(2, C), on V_i are the integers m such that −m_i ≤ m ≤ m_i." URL as above.

## 4. ρ∨ is regular, and so is e

- **QUOTED.** MIT 18.757 Lecture 17, proof of Lemma 17.1: "Since ρ∨ is regular, the 0-weight space of this module (the centralizer Z_g(ρ∨)) is h, i.e., has dimension r." The same lecture says "ρ∨ is an integral coweight", so exp(2πiρ∨) = 1. URL: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-757-representations-of-lie-groups-fall-2023/mit18_757_f23_lec17.pdf
- **PROVED.** That ρ∨ is regular semisimple also follows directly from §3: α(ρ∨) = height(α) ≠ 0 for every root α. Integrality means the one-parameter group exp(2πitρ∨) closes at t = 1, so "the principal circle" is a genuine closed circle in the adjoint group.
- **QUOTED.** Baraglia: "We have that the element e is regular, that is it has an l-dimensional centralizer spanned by elements e1, . . . , el." MIT, Lemma 17.2: "The element e = Σ e_i is regular."
- **QUOTED (uniqueness).** Dalakov: "'most' of the sl(2, C)-subalgebras of a simple Lie algebra g are principal, and any two principal subalgebras are conjugate." Hitchin: "A principal 3-dimensional subalgebra defines a homomorphism from SL(2, C) to G^c and hence from SU(2) to a compact real form G of G^c."

## 5. Exponents, Coxeter number, and "the most economical" decomposition

- **QUOTED.** Dalakov, §6.2: "The adjoint representation of an sl(2, C)-subalgebra a ⊂ g decomposes g into a sum of irreducible representations, whose number is at least l = rk(g) (Theorem 5.2, ibid.). … The minimal number of summands (l) is achieved precisely when a is principal, and in that case they are all odd-dimensional (Corollary 5.2, ibid.). The dimensions of the summands are determined by the exponents of g". "ibid." here is Kostant 1959.
- **QUOTED.** Baraglia: "arrange the positive roots of g into an array with the k-th row consisting of all roots of height k, filling in rows from right to left. Then the lengths of the columns from left to right are the exponents. In particular we see that if the highest root δ has height M then m_l = M is the largest exponent. … the dimensions of V_1, . . . , V_l are (2m_1 + 1), . . . , (2m_l + 1)".
- **QUOTED.** MIT, Exercise 17.5(ii): "Show that m_1 = 1 and Σ m_i = |R+|. … the conjugate partition μ†_g is (n_1, ..., n_{h−1}), where n_i is the number of positive roots α of height i (i.e., (ρ∨, α) = i) and h := m_r + 1. Conclude that h = (ρ∨, θ) + 1 where θ is the maximal root, i.e., the Coxeter number of g." It also says "m_i = d_i − 1, where d_i are the degrees of g."
- **QUOTED (Coxeter element).** Baraglia, §2.2: "g = exp(2πix/h) where h = M + 1 is the Coxeter number. … Kostant [10] shows that g acts on this Cartan subalgebra as a Coxeter element for the Weyl group."
- **QUOTED.** Wikipedia, "Coxeter number": "If the highest root is Σm_iα_i for simple roots α_i, then the Coxeter number is 1+Σm_i." URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_number
- **Source discrepancy, recorded.** Dalakov §6.1 writes "The natural number M is the Coxeter number of g" for the top height M. Baraglia, MIT and Wikipedia all give h = M + 1. Dalakov's own sl(3) example has −2 ≤ m ≤ 2 (M = 2), while the Coxeter number of A₂ is 3. I follow h = M + 1.
- **PROVED (SU(3)).** For A₂ the exponents are 1 and 2, h = 3, and the highest root θ = α₁ + α₂ has height 2. Under ad ρ∨ the eigenvalues on the roots are ±1 (on α₁, α₂) and ±2 (on θ). **So the principal circle in SU(3) carries charges 1 and 2, not only 1.**

## 6. The principal SU(2) in gauge theory: what is actually in the literature

### 6a. Monopoles: the principal embedding gives Toda (QUOTED)

- Bais, §7.1, on spherically symmetric monopoles with J = L + T, "T generates some SU(2) subgroup of the full gauge group": "If one chooses the minimal regular embedding corresponding to the (simple) roots of the full gauge algebra one obtains the fundamental monopoles discussed before. The choice of a non-regular embedding leads to more interesting results. For example, the choice of the maximal or better principal SU(2) embedding, where the fundamental (vector) representation branches to a single irreducible SU(2) representation leads to one- (two-) dimensional conformally invariant Toda-like systems". F. A. Bais, "To be or not to be? Magnetic monopoles in non-abelian gauge theories", arXiv:hep-th/0407197. URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/hep-th/0407197
- **QUOTED (bibliographic only).** Ganoulis, Goddard and Olive, "Self-dual monopoles and Toda molecules", Nucl. Phys. B205 (1982) 601–636. This is from the Olive bibliography in arXiv:2009.05849, surfaced by search. **UNVERIFIED** as to its contents: I did not read it.

### 6b. Higgs bundles and Hitchin's equations: the principal subgroup is the backbone (QUOTED)

- Baraglia: "Higgs bundles were introduced by Hitchin in [8] after considering dimensional reduction of the Yang-Mills self-duality equations to a surface." URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6421
- Hitchin (1992): "To prove Theorem A for an arbitrary simple Lie group we make essential use of the results of Kostant on the principal 3-dimensional subgroup. The split real form of this gives the homomorphism from PSL(2, R) to G^r which embeds Teichmüller space in the Teichmüller component."
- Baraglia (abstract): cyclic Higgs bundles in the Hitchin component are "equivalent to a certain class of solutions to the affine Toda equations". In the introduction, such a Higgs field is "cyclic as an element of the Lie algebra in the sense of Kostant".
- **Reading (no new claim).** This is self-dual Yang–Mills reduced to two dimensions, with the principal SU(2) singled out by uniformization of the surface. It is not a statement about the Yang–Mills Hessian on a three-manifold.

### 6c. Stability of Yang–Mills on S²: the Hessian is governed by charges under a circle (QUOTED; the closest analogue to Helike I found)

- Zhang, Horvathy and Rawnsley, abstract: "The stability problem of non-Abelian monopoles with respect to 'Brandt–Neri–Coleman type' variations reduces to that of a pure gauge theory on the two-sphere. Each topological sector admits exactly one stable monopole charge, and each unstable monopole admits 2 Σ(2|q| − 1) negative modes, where the sum goes over the negative eigenvalues q of an operator related to the non-Abelian charge Q of Goddard, Nuyts and Olive." P.-M. Zhang, P. A. Horvathy, J. Rawnsley, "Topology, and (in)stability of non-Abelian monopoles", arXiv:1102.1940. URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1102.1940
- Same paper, §1: "This is just a special case of YM on a Riemann surface, studied by Atiyah and Bott [20]." And: "It follows that a monopole is stable if its only eigenvalues are 0 or ±1/2 [7, 10, 3]. All other monopoles correspond to saddle points".
- Same paper, §5: "the only possibility for getting negative modes is to have q ≤ −1 and j = |q| − 1, in which case δ²E = m² − |q|(a, a) < 0. … the monopole is unstable if and only if there is an eigenvalue such that |q| ≥ 1."
- Same paper, §6, ties this to the Hopf bundle under my Berger spheres: "a Dirac monopole of unit strength and is given by the Hopf fibration S3 → S2. Now χ(e^{it}) = exp[4πiQt] is a homomorphism of U(1) into H allowing us to form the associated bundle". And: "the negative modes are the holomorphic sections of ad P ⊗ Ω^(1,0)." The Chern class of the α-block is "2α(Q) − 2 = n_α".
- **UNVERIFIED.** Atiyah–Bott 1983 (Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A). The publisher page returned HTTP 403, so I cite it only through Zhang–Horvathy–Rawnsley's reference to it.
- **PROVED, modulo normalization.** Combine ZHR's criterion (negative modes need |q| ≥ 1, and the deepest one sits in the largest-|q| block, since δ²E = m² − |q|) with §3 (ad ρ∨ has eigenvalue height(α) on g_α). If the monopole charge Q is taken along the principal circle, the charges q on the adjoint are proportional to root heights. **The deepest descent direction then lies in the highest-root block (height h − 1), not in a height-1 block.** Under Brandt–Neri the stable charge has adjoint eigenvalues only in {0, ±½}, which is a minimal-coweight circle, not the principal one. On S² the principal circle is not where stability lives. If anything it is the circle with the most negative modes, for a given overall scale.

### 6d. Instantons: the standard embedding is the minimal (long-root) SU(2), not the principal one

- **QUOTED (secondary, search snippet only, so treat as UNVERIFIED).** Search results summarizing arXiv:2512.23041 say Bernard–Christ–Guth–Weinberg (Phys. Rev. D 16 (1977) 2967) embed the BPST solution in the SU(2) of a long root, to minimize the charge. I did not extract that sentence verbatim.
- **QUOTED.** arXiv:hep-th/0205162 ("Twisting E8 Five-Branes"; I did not record its authors): for the SU(2) in SO(4) ⊂ SO(5), "we find that k = 1, 2, 6, respectively. A charge 1 instanton embedded in this SU(2) subgroup has the smallest charge possible". URL: https://arxiv.org/pdf/hep-th/0205162. The point I take from it: different SU(2) embeddings carry different instanton charges.
- **UNVERIFIED.** I found no source placing a basic instanton or a Yang–Mills critical point on S³ along the principal SU(2). A search for "Yang–Mills critical points on S³ … principal embedding … Morse index" returned nothing relevant. The closest hits were Råde's invariant-connection compactness papers (arXiv:math/0004041, dg-ga/9704007), and I did not read those for this point.

## 8. Verdict on the bridge "she lives on the principal circle" (literature side only)

1. **The structural half holds (PROVED from QUOTED facts).** H = diag(1,0,−1) is ρ∨ of SU(3). ρ∨ is the principal grading element: it is regular, integral, and grades by root height. The SU(3) Test D circle is the principal circle. In SU(2) every circle is conjugate to the principal one, so SU(2) tells us nothing either way.
2. **The literature does not supply the bridge.** In gauge theory the principal SU(2) shows up where self-duality (Bogomol'nyi, Hitchin equations) reduces to Toda, with the Cartan matrix and Coxeter structure appearing. That is integrability, not the Hessian floor. Where the literature does compute a Yang–Mills Hessian against a circle in G (Brandt–Neri–Coleman, ZHR, Atiyah–Bott on S²), the governing data are the **charges of the adjoint blocks under the circle**. Instability sits in the **largest-charge** block, and the stable circle is minimal, not principal.
3. **There is a confound C vs D cannot separate (IMAGINED, but it follows from §4).** The hypercharge generator ∝ diag(1,1,−2) is **singular**: its centralizer is U(2). ρ∨ is **regular**. So Test C failing and Test D succeeding (hypothetically; no D result is stated here) would fit "regular circle" just as well as "principal circle". Many regular circles are not principal, e.g. diag(2,1,−3) in SU(3), or any generic diagonal element. **Discriminating test (to pre-register):** stretch along a regular but non-principal circle. If she reappears, the law is "regular", not "principal". In SU(4) or Sp(2), the arc note's proposed test, both kinds of circle are available.
4. **Charge bookkeeping caution (PROVED, from §5).** Under ρ∨, SU(3) has charge-1 blocks (simple roots) and a charge-2 block (the highest root). "Her charge is exactly one" therefore places her in a simple-root block, not the top block. In a general group, ρ∨ charges run up to h − 1, the arc note's "how tall the highest root is". The ZHR mechanism points the other way: the deepest descent goes to the largest charge. Whether the stretched-sphere floor follows the same block logic is open, and in this analogy it is a warning sign for the bridge.
5. **Overall label:** "She lives on the principal circle (and nowhere else), in any compact simple group" is **IMAGINED**. The literature supports its premises (what the principal circle is and why it is distinguished). It offers no mechanism tying the Hessian floor to principality. The closest analogue, Hessian negative modes against a circle in G, picks out charge size and not principality. That makes the regular-vs-principal control the first thing to run.
