Reading the Curve, Not Counting the Points: A Consistent, Stable Petrov–Galerkin Curvature Force for Discrete Yang–Mills Dynamics on the Poincaré Homology Sphere
Bee Rosa Davis, Davis Geometric. November 2026. Version 2.

On a mesh you never see a field whole, only its integrals along edges and its fluxes through faces. The standard way to compute a gauge field's force from those numbers, the lowest-order Whitney reading, treats each little cell as if it were flat. On the Poincaré homology sphere S3/2I, a space that is curved everywhere and has no boundary for a flat-cell assumption to hide behind, that reading's force error stops falling at 26% however fine the mesh gets. An error that will not shrink is not a resolution problem. It means the description is wrong at every scale. This paper shows that the floor comes from how curvature is represented, not from the data, and removes it: read the curvature from the cell's neighbours instead of assuming it flat, and count the clues before trusting the fit.

The two-ring reading

  • Count the clues. A closed linear curvature on a cell has 11 unknowns. One ring of neighbouring faces supplies exactly 11 readings, so the fit must pass through all of them, noise included: it amplifies rough data by a median 125–252, and the resulting operator has 489 of 961 physical modes growing instead of decaying at n = 2. Two rings supply 26–29 readings for the same 11 unknowns. The fit becomes an equality-constrained least-squares vote, still exact on closed linear curvature, and the amplification settles to a mesh-uniform median of 30.5–35.2.
  • Accurate. Tested against the original Whitney variations, the two-ring force is consistent. Its error falls to 6.22%, 3.83%, 2.79% and 1.45% at mesh levels n = 1, 2, 3, 5 (observed orders 0.73, 0.80, 1.29) and passes the unchanged BW05 convergence gate. The 26% floor is gone.
  • Stable. Every decomposed physical eigenvalue (n ≤ 3) lies in the right half-plane, the smallest at the control's true value of 4; at n = 1, 2 the symmetric part is positive definite, so the semi-discrete flow is dissipative.
  • Nothing added. Nothing is fitted and no data are added: only the size of the neighbourhood the reading consults changes. Two to four well-chosen second-ring cells per cell already suffice; the same number chosen at random is unstable.
  • One honest cost. The force is not the gradient of an energy, and the paper shows quantitatively why every symmetrized variant fails. An accurate, stable and reciprocal force is left open.

Into SU(2)

For the non-Abelian group the readings must first be brought into register: each neighbouring holonomy is carried onto the cell's own fibre by link transport before anything is compared. With that, the covariant recovery converges at second order on the Levi-Civita fields of the round and a Berger metric. On the Berger field the native SU(2) force stays 25.5% wrong at every level, while the two-ring force falls to 2.51% at n = 5 and passes BW05. Its linearised spectrum has no rough modes (the one-ring force has 207, then 1,451), and its numerical range, which bounds transient growth, has its left edge at −4.66 at n = 2, against −4.68 for the native force and −19,993 for one ring.

Letting the lattice move

The harder test lets the links move. The Berger metric (3, 1, 1) follows its Ricci flow over t ∈ [0, 0.02]; the links start exact and evolve under the force; at the end they are compared with the exact links of the flowed metric.

  • The result. The two-ring force ends 13.8%, 1.76% and 0.87% off the exact trajectory (error over the distance travelled) at n = 1, 2, 3. The native force ends 7.01%, 2.21% and 1.07%: native leads on the coarsest mesh, and two rings lead from n = 2 on.
  • The control. Freezing the metric at its starting value leaves an error near 8% that does not shrink with the mesh, so the lattice demonstrably follows the moving background.
  • Said plainly. Most of the field's motion over this window happens even with the metric frozen. Measured against only the part caused by the moving metric, the two-ring error is 10.6% at n = 3, down from 18–28% at n = 2: falling, and a tenth rather than a hundredth.
  • A prediction that failed, and why. The native force was expected to stall in time, as its instantaneous error stalls near 25%. It did not. A first-order filter model, which predicts every measured trajectory error to within 6.1%, explains why: 99% of the native error at n = 2 lies in stiff directions that the dynamics damps within the window. A second forecast, that two rings would pull further ahead at n = 3, also missed: both errors halved.
  • The step. The finest level uses the Lie implicit midpoint rule built on the Cayley map, which keeps every update exactly on SU(2). It comes from the companion paper Three Ways Across a Turn, where the Crank–Nicolson step is the tangent reading of a turn. Before use it reproduced the fourth-order runs at n = 1, 2 to within 1.5% with eight steps.

What is new in version 2

  • The SU(2) numerical range; the flow in time at n = 1, 2, 3; the filter model and the measured explanation of the failed stall prediction; the Cayley time step; the exact face-defect derivative.
  • A second check of every flow result: the force was rewritten to work on all cells at once, under a specification committed before the new code ran, and gated on parity with the original code (native gradient identical to the last bit, two-ring within 5.4 × 10−14). Every Cayley run was repeated on it and reproduces its committed result to 5 × 10−14 or better, with the same Newton iteration counts. A two-ring force evaluation at n = 3 went from 89 s to 0.69 s.
  • Corrections after an integrity review. Because so many results are now assumed to be faked, I had this work reviewed for exactly that, by review agents in my own workflow (not peer review), each told to look for ways the code could flatter the result. They found none: the evolving lattice never sees the answer it is scored against, the exact reference is a closed-form formula, no failed run is hidden, no criterion was loosened to turn a failure into a pass, and about 120 numbers recompute from the saved receipts. They found places where the text said more than the record shows, and three roundings that leaned my way. This version fixes all of them: it states that only the Abelian path runs in the engine, reports the original control verdicts beside the corrected ones, names the specifications that were committed together with their results, states the one tolerance relaxed after a failure, and prints the honest roundings.
  • A new subtitle (the paper now covers SU(2), not only an Abelian control), a shorter abstract, two new figures, and 30 pages.

What is claimed, and what is not

  • Every number traces to a saved receipt; each experiment has a written specification with its pass and fail lines, and the paper says where the commit history shows it came first and where it does not. Failures are reported beside successes.
  • No Ricci-flow claim and no mass-gap claim. The targets are the instantaneous Yang–Mills force and, for SU(2), one short window of the flow on one metric. These results are a prerequisite for a lattice Davis–Wilson correspondence, not an instance of one, and the programme's release gate stays open. Whether any of this bears on the Yang–Mills mass gap is the Clay problem, and nothing here claims it.
  • The SU(2) recovery, force and flow are research scripts, not engine verbs. The flow is measured at n ≤ 3; stability and the numerical range at n ≤ 2.

The code

Everything runs on GIGI, my open-source geometric engine (PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0): github.com/nurdymuny/gigi, tag reading-the-curve-v2. For the Abelian control, native Rust transport and mass assembly are checked against the full 120-fold cover, and the two-ring recovery is a native query verb, RECOVER_CURVATURE, that matches the research code cell by cell on every mesh:

RECOVER_CURVATURE cells FROM edges ON field RINGS 2;

The engine stays in the repository; this record carries the research code and data behind the paper.

Why this problem

I came to this work through Grigori Perelman. His proof of the Poincaré conjecture by Ricci flow made me want to understand what the geometry was actually doing, and this paper lives on the space that conjecture is about: Poincaré's own homology sphere, with its metric moving under Ricci flow while a gauge field moves on top of it. The fix itself came from a physical intuition about how parts snap together: magnets, not clamps. Let the neighbourhood draw the fit into register instead of forcing it, and then count the clues, because an intuition does not tell you how far the pull has to reach.

Companion paper

Three Ways Across a Turn: Chord, Arc and Tangent (B. R. Davis, doi:10.5281/zenodo.24375338) reads one turn three ways: the chord, which lattice gauge theory has used since Wilson; the arc, which the continuum uses; and the outside path along the tangents, whose leg is the Cayley parameter. It explains why the chord–arc gap is negligible on these meshes, and it supplies the Cayley time step that carried this paper's flow to its finest level.
