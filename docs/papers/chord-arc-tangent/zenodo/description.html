One turn, three ways across

Turn a line of length one through an angle θ. Its tip moves along a circle, and afterwards the two tip positions can be joined in three natural ways: by the straight chord, by the arc the turn actually traced, and by the outside path along the two tangent lines that meet beyond the circle. Their lengths are 2 sin(θ/2), θ and 2 tan(θ/2), always in that order. All of this is classical, from Archimedes through Snellius and Huygens. This paper reads the three paths as one object, three measurements of the same turn that miss in fixed proportions, and follows that proportion through length, energy, algebra and time stepping, then into the gauge group SU(2) and onto a real lattice.

The three readings

  • Length. The chord falls short of the arc by θ3/24 and the outside path overshoots by θ3/12: exactly twice as much, at leading order. The Snellius–Huygens blend (2C + T)/3 therefore misses only by θ5/320.
  • Energy. Read the turn as a complex number U = eiθ. The chord is U − 1, and its square is Wilson's lattice energy; the arc is the logarithm, and its square is the continuum (Manton) energy to leading order; the outside path gives an energy that overshoots by twice as much. The same 2:1 weights cancel the θ4 term exactly: (2C2 + T2)/3 = θ2 + 7θ6/720 + …, using only arithmetic on U.
  • Algebra. The tangent leg tan(θ/2) is the Cayley parameter that rebuilds U with plain arithmetic, U = (1 + iτ)/(1 − iτ). It reaches every turn except the half turn U = −1, where the two tangents become parallel and the outside path breaks.
  • Time stepping. On the simplest turning motion, y′ = iy, explicit Euler steps out along the tangent and spirals outward, implicit Euler spirals inward on the mirror spiral, and Crank–Nicolson, which is the Cayley map, stays exactly on the circle and lags by s3/12: the same one-twelfth that separates the detour from the arc. The outward spiral is logarithmic, and at exactly one step size (s* ≈ 0.6515, 33.08° per step) it is the golden spiral.

The non-Abelian turn

For a unit quaternion U = cos a + n sin a, an element of SU(2), everything carries over with θ replaced by the geodesic angle a on the three-sphere: |U − 1| = 2 sin(a/2), log U = a n, and the Cayley vector v = Im U/(1 + Re U) has length tan(a/2). Geometrically, the Cayley vector is the stereographic projection of U from −1. Half the chord is the distance d in the double-cover identity S + d2 = 1 of The Double Cover Principle, with sameness S = cos2(θ/2) in the sense of The Geometry of Sameness.

Twins, the far side, and Bertrand

Every miss has a twin on the other side. Snellius' lower estimate misses by θ5/2880, nine times less than the upper one, and the 9:1 blend of the twins is exact to θ7. A chord does not know which way round a turn went; the outside path does. Crossing the circle's rim makes the turn imaginary, yet S + d2 = 1 survives. Bertrand's paradox turns out to be the question of which of these quantities is taken to be uniform: the arc (probability 1/3), √S (1/2), or S (1/4); a uniformly random turn of SU(2) gives a fourth answer, 1/3 − √3/(4π) ≈ 0.1955.

The ticket game

A game puts it all in one room. A ticket buys one straight walk of at most one step; two players each walk one ticket out from the centre. Getting back costs one ticket along the curve up to one radian (57.30°), straight across up to 60°, and, counted as energy the way the lattice counts it, exactly one ticket at 90° (Pythagoras). With whole tickets every crossing is two trips, turning either at the centre or at its twin corner, which lands on the circle at 120° and meets the tangent corner at 90°.

On a real lattice

On the SU(2) meshes of the Poincaré homology sphere used in Reading the Curve, every face turns by at most 0.094 radians, so the chord and the arc differ in energy by under 0.08%. A small energy gap does not by itself bound the gap between forces, so the force was measured directly: reading the arc instead of the chord in the force's recovery changes the SU(2) force by 0.090%, 0.0084% and 0.0021% of its norm at n = 1, 2, 3, and its error (4.7–36%) by at most 0.011 percentage points. The chord-versus-arc choice is not what limits that work. Where the three paths do matter is in time: the flow of Reading the Curve uses the Cayley step at its finest level, where eight steps reproduce fine fourth-order runs to within 1.5%. A section maps each idea to where it lives in GIGI and its research scripts, and states which pieces are engine code and which are not.

Toward Yang–Mills, labelled speculative

In a gauge field every small loop carries a turn, its holonomy, and the picture reads directly: the turn creates the energy, and a lattice pays for it along the chord. The last section keeps the exact and the speculative apart. Exact: the group of turns SU(2) is curved, with sectional curvature ¼|[X, Y]|2 (SU(3) is curved too, but not a round sphere: it has flat planes of commuting directions), and the energy of a turn, 1 − cos θ, takes arbitrarily small values, so curvature alone puts no floor under the energy. Speculative: in the ticket game a rule makes the ticket indivisible, and only with such an added assumption would a floor appear.

What is claimed, and what is not

  • Every identity is checked symbolically or numerically by verify.py: 68 of 68 checks pass. The face angles are measured from frozen mesh data by face_angles.py.
  • No priority is claimed for any classical identity. Parts of the energy story are known: cancelling the θ4 term of Wilson's action has been done for U(1) with an adjoint term (de Forcrand and coauthors); the tangent energy is Lüscher's topology-conserving action at its boundary value; the scalar inequality behind the cancellation is of Wilker–Huygens type; and chord, arc and tangent appear side by side as discrete curvatures of polygons (Crane and Wardetzky). What I have not found is the chord–tangent blend proposed as a lattice energy, its algebraic form in U, or its coefficients 7/720 and 1/1008.
  • The paper does not claim that the blend is a better lattice action (that needs simulations and a treatment of the half-turn barrier), nor that it explains any error in the curvature-recovery work.
  • The paper does not claim to resolve the Yang–Mills mass gap, which remains an open Clay problem, and the ticket game does not supply it.
  • Open: how the blend compares in non-Abelian simulations with the adjoint-improved and barrier actions, and whether its barrier at U = −1 inherits their known costs; a Cayley step inside the engine itself; a strong-field or coarse-lattice test, the only regime where the blend could matter.

What is new in version 2.1

Version 1 was posted on 19 November 2026. Version 2.1 adds the corrections made since, including those from two adversarial reviews.

  • The Yang–Mills section, separated. It is now titled speculative and split in two: the exact statements (the curvature of SU(2); 1 − cos θ is continuous and arbitrarily small, so curvature alone gives no floor) and a motivating picture in which indivisibility is stated as an added assumption. A status paragraph says what the Clay problem concerns. SU(3) is described correctly as curved but not round.
  • The force, measured directly. Version 1 inferred from the small energy gap that the chord-versus-arc choice could not matter for the lattice force. Version 2.1 measures it (0.090%, 0.0084% and 0.0021% of the force at n = 1, 2, 3) and gives the force errors as 4.7–36%, also corrected in Figure 8(b).
  • The prior-art search, done. Version 1 listed it as open. The related work is now cited (adjoint-improved actions, Lüscher's barrier actions and Creutz's objection to them, Wilker–Huygens inequalities, discrete curvatures), and the claim is narrowed to what was not found.
  • Stated with their domains. The Manton energy is the arc's square to leading order; the chord's half-angle direction holds for 0 < θ < 2π and is not by itself a double-cover construction; the two circles of the ticket game meet in two points for 0 < θ < π.
  • Reproducible from the repository alone. The per-face angles behind the face-angle figure are now a committed receipt, so the figure rebuilds without the meshes. The source, figures and scripts now come as one LaTeX package that compiles as is.
  • The Russian edition is updated to match, section by section.

Why they are here. I came to this work through Grigori Perelman. His proof of the Poincaré conjecture by Ricci flow made me want to understand what the geometry was actually doing, and that question led me, over time, to the wider tradition of Soviet-era mathematics: its problem books, its habit of making a small construction carry a large idea, and its care about what has been established and what has not. This paper's subject, three ways across a single turn, is that kind of small construction. Publishing it in Russian, alongside the chapter in which I thank Mr. Perelman, is my way of returning the work to the language of the tradition that inspired it.

The chapter. Thank You, Mr. Perelman is a first-person account of how I came to that tradition through Perelman's work on Ricci flow. It works through square constructions from Delone and Zhitomirsky's geometry problem book, parallel transport around a loop, and a group computation in GIGI, and it keeps a strict line between what is proved and what remains open. It includes the lattice results of Reading the Curve: a lattice force that first failed its convergence test converges once curvature is read from two rings of neighbouring cells, and the SU(2) flow follows a metric moving under Ricci flow. The lattice correspondence as a whole is stated as still open, and the chapter makes no claim to a new proof of the Poincaré conjecture.
